# Influence and technical leadership

Technical leadership creates shared understanding and coordinated action across people who hold different context and authority.

## Why it matters

Cross-team changes fail when agreement is assumed, incentives are ignored, or implementation knowledge stays with one advocate.

## How it works

Map stakeholders, decisions, incentives, and missing context. Listen before proposing, write concise artifacts, expose tradeoffs, and ask for explicit commitments. Adapt depth to the audience without changing facts. Disagreement becomes productive when claims, evidence, values, and decision ownership are separated.

Influence is built through credible mechanisms and reciprocal understanding, not message volume. A decision map distinguishes who decides, who supplies expertise, who executes, and who bears consequences. Pre-reading and small-group discovery surface concerns, while the shared forum records tradeoffs openly. Commitment names owner, action, and evidence rather than vague alignment.

## See it yourself

Explain a storage migration three ways. The operator needs failure modes, dashboards, and rollback; the product lead needs user risk and sequencing; the executive needs strategic consequence and decision required. Ask each listener to state the decision and their responsibility. If answers differ, communication failed despite polished slides. The facts remain constant while relevance changes.

## Where it shows up

During a reliability standard rollout, technical leaders gather service constraints, publish a testable proposal, pilot it with willing teams, and use incident evidence to refine defaults. They secure commitments from platform and service owners separately. Mentoring others to run reviews prevents the standard from depending on one person's presence.

## When it breaks

Consensus becomes a veto, status replaces evidence, private alignment excludes affected teams, and heroic ownership prevents growth. When alignment fails, first list unresolved claims, evidence, value tradeoffs, decision owner, and unrepresented affected groups. A factual dispute needs data; a priority dispute needs authority; missing trust needs listening and follow-through, not another architecture diagram.

## Practice

**Build:** create a stakeholder and decision map, written proposal, and explicit commitment log for a risky change. **Break:** omit one burdened team and introduce a values conflict; detect and repair the process. **Explain back:** deliver audience-specific versions, then use listener teach-back as observable proof of shared understanding.

## Check yourself

1. What requires consensus versus consultation?
2. How do you make disagreement legible?
3. When does helping become a bottleneck?
